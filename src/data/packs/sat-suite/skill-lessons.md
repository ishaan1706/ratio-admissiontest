# SAT skill lessons

Read the approach, cover the working and try both examples, then open the matching bank skill. The 30 labels below match the bank; Command of Evidence is split into textual and quantitative.

## Central Ideas and Details

**Concept and strategy.** For the main idea, combine subject + what the passage says about it. For a detail, go back to the sentence that answers the exact question. Recheck time, group, and exception words.

**Worked examples.** A clinic’s evening appointment trial shortened waits without increasing appointment slots. Main idea: changing when slots were available improved waits, not how many slots existed. If a bird survey says nesting rose only near covered ponds, the supported detail is an increase near covered ponds—not across all ponds.

**Watch for:** A vivid example that is accurate but too narrow to summarize the text.

## Command of Evidence: Textual

**Concept and strategy.** Break a claim into pieces, such as “improved speed without reducing accuracy.” A quotation or result should directly support all the important pieces.

**Worked examples.** A timed repair fell from 18 to 12 minutes and passed the same inspection tests. That supports both speed and accuracy; “everyone liked the method” supports neither. To support “the narrator dreads praise but welcomes useful feedback,” choose an excerpt where the narrator avoids applause yet asks what to change.

**Watch for:** An answer that echoes a keyword while describing the wrong comparison or mechanism.

## Command of Evidence: Quantitative

**Concept and strategy.** Convert the claim into a row, column, and calculation. Identify whether the requested value is a level, change, proportion, or comparison; check units.

**Worked examples.** Route A: 16 riders before, 24 after. Route B: 30 before, 35 after. A increased more (8 vs. 5), even though B ended higher. If poll support moves from 40% to 50%, that is 10 percentage points or 25% growth relative to the original 40%, not 10% growth.

**Watch for:** Comparing final values when the claim is about changes.

## Inferences

**Concept and strategy.** Choose the smallest new claim the text supports. Write down the given facts first, then identify the link between them. Preserve uncertainty when a stronger causal claim has not been tested.

**Worked examples.** A bookstore adds a side door; visitors enter faster and floor area is unchanged. An additional entry may ease access; there is no evidence it increased sales. Two surveys use the same list, which excludes seasonal workers. Matching findings need not establish what those workers think.

**Watch for:** Explanations you know outside the passage or unsupported words like “all” and “guarantees.”

## Words in Context

**Concept and strategy.** Determine the required part of speech and predict a plain-language substitute. Check the words before and after the blank for contrast or cause. Reread the complete sentence.

**Worked examples.** “The wet cloth did not stop the sound, but it ___ it.” “Weakened” points to “attenuated,” not “eliminated.” “The separate invoices ___ the initial estimate by giving matching totals.” “Supported” points to “corroborated,” not “contradicted.”

**Watch for:** The everyday sense of a word when the context calls for a specialized sense.

## Text Structure and Purpose

**Concept and strategy.** Reduce the passage to a sequence of jobs: introduce, illustrate, qualify, or conclude. For a sentence-function question, connect the sentence to the claim around it, not just to what it says.

**Worked examples.** “Some guides call the route flat. Measurements reveal a steep final kilometer.” Sentence two qualifies the first description. A passage beginning with a surprising museum layout and ending with why it limits crowding aims to explain a design choice, not to describe every room.

**Watch for:** A relevant topic stated as if it were the author’s purpose.

## Cross-Text Connections

**Concept and strategy.** Summarize each author’s position separately, then compare the claims at the same level: evidence, method, conclusion, or scope.

**Worked examples.** Text 1 recommends fans after a sunny-site trial. Text 2 finds little effect on shaded sites but accepts the sunny result. Text 2 limits the recommendation, not the result. One designer values familiar signage and another values novelty; both can still agree that clear wayfinding matters.

**Watch for:** Attributing Text 1’s claim to Text 2 or mistaking a qualification for complete disagreement.

## Rhetorical Synthesis

**Concept and strategy.** Start with the goal and audience. Mark the notes needed to fulfill it. Check the answer for fidelity and for whether it actually makes the requested comparison or introduction.

**Worked examples.** Notes: Iva made a model in 2008, revised it in 2015, and taught design. To emphasize timing, write “Seven years after making the model, Iva revised it.” To introduce Iva’s occupation to unfamiliar readers, write “Iva taught design,” not a sentence about model dates alone.

**Watch for:** The longest choice or the one with the most facts; relevance is the scoring criterion.

## Transitions

**Concept and strategy.** Read both sides before looking at options. Identify addition, contrast, cause/result, example, restatement, substitute, or time. Check the direction of that relationship.

**Worked examples.** “The train stopped. ___ the travelers took a bus.” “Instead” signals a substitute. “The lamp produced enough visible light. ___ it emitted too little UV for the test.” “However” signals a contrast between two criteria.

**Watch for:** Picking “therefore” because the sentences are related, even though the second is not a consequence.

## Boundaries

**Concept and strategy.** Find each subject and finite verb. Determine whether each clause can stand alone. Check whether an interruption is essential, and pair its opening and closing punctuation if needed.

**Worked examples.** “The doors opened; the lights stayed off.” Both clauses can stand alone. “The clock, which had stopped at noon, was repaired.” Remove the interrupting clause: “The clock was repaired” still works.

**Watch for:** A comma splice before a transition: use “; however,” between complete clauses, not “, however,” alone.

## Form, Structure, and Sense

**Concept and strategy.** Check subject–verb agreement, timeline, pronoun reference and possession, modifier attachment, and parallel forms. Temporarily cross out distracting prepositional phrases.

**Worked examples.** “The bundle of wires fits” because bundle is singular. “Holding the loose cable, Amira tightened the clip” attaches the modifier to the person holding it. “To label, sort, and archive” keeps a parallel series.

**Watch for:** Treating the noun closest to the blank as the subject or placing a modifier next to an object that cannot do the action.

## Linear equations in one variable

**Concept and strategy.** Transform both sides equally. Clear fixed denominators, distribute signs, collect like terms, isolate, substitute to check.

**Worked examples.** 4(x − 2) = 20 gives x − 2 = 5, so x = 7. For (x + 1)/3 − (x − 2)/2 = 1, multiplying by 6 gives 2x + 2 − 3x + 6 = 6, so x = 2.

**Watch for:** Applying the denominator multiplier only to terms on one side.

## Linear equations in two variables

**Concept and strategy.** An ordered pair satisfies a line if it makes the equation true. For intercepts, set the other coordinate to zero; use rise/run for slope.

**Worked examples.** 2x + 3y = 18 crosses the x-axis at (9, 0) and y-axis at (0, 6). The triangle with those intercepts has area 27. For y = 4x − 3, a point with x = 5 has y = 17.

**Watch for:** Giving a y-axis crossing with y = 0 instead of x = 0.

## Linear functions

**Concept and strategy.** A linear function gives the same output change for each equal input change. Identify slope and initial value, with units.

**Worked examples.** A rental costs $11 up front and $4/hour: C(h) = 11 + 4h, so C(3) = $23. If f(2) = 9 and f(5) = 21, the slope is (21 − 9)/(5 − 2) = 4, so f(0) = 1.

**Watch for:** Treating a measured value at nonzero input as the intercept.

## Systems of two linear equations in two variables

**Concept and strategy.** A solution satisfies both equations. Substitute when one variable is isolated; eliminate when coefficients are easy to match. On a graph, inspect intersections.

**Worked examples.** x + y = 13 and 2x + y = 20 give x = 7 by subtraction and y = 6. The lines y = 3x + 2 and y = 3x − 4 have equal slopes and different intercepts, so no intersection and no solution.

**Watch for:** Stopping at a coordinate that satisfies only one equation or assuming every pair of lines intersects.

## Linear inequalities in one or two variables

**Concept and strategy.** Translate “at least” to ≥ and “at most” to ≤. Reverse the sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative. Check a point or number to verify the feasible side.

**Worked examples.** 7 − 2x ≥ 13 gives −2x ≥ 6, or x ≤ −3. With 2x + y ≤ 6, (1, 3) qualifies (5 ≤ 6) and (3, 2) does not (8 > 6).

**Watch for:** Rounding a whole-number solution in the wrong direction or including a strict boundary.

## Equivalent expressions

**Concept and strategy.** Equivalent expressions agree for every input in the permitted domain. Simplify by distributing, factoring, or applying exponent rules; don’t assume one matching substitution proves equivalence.

**Worked examples.** (x + 6)(x − 2) = x² + 4x − 12. For x ≠ 0, x⁵/x² = x³; the simplification does not make x = 0 valid for the original fraction.

**Watch for:** Combining exponents on a sum or canceling terms across addition.

## Nonlinear equations in one variable and systems of equations in two variables

**Concept and strategy.** Identify the structure (factorable quadratic, radical, intersection, etc.). Solve and test each candidate in the original equation, especially after squaring.

**Worked examples.** x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0 gives 2 and 3. For y = x² and y = 3x + 4, the equal-height equation factors as (x − 4)(x + 1) = 0; intersection x-values are −1 and 4.

**Watch for:** Reporting one root when two are requested, or keeping an extraneous squared solution.

## Nonlinear functions

**Concept and strategy.** Recognize patterns: quadratic vertex/zeros, exponential constant multiplier, or other features. Separate input, output and units.

**Worked examples.** f(x) = 2(x − 3)² − 5 has minimum −5 at x = 3. A tank starts at 80 units and loses 25% every hour; after two hours it has 80(0.75)² = 45 units.

**Watch for:** Confusing vertex x with vertex y or subtracting a fixed amount in a percent model.

## Ratios, rates, proportional relationships, and units

**Concept and strategy.** Write units with the numbers and cancel unwanted units. In a proportional relationship the ratio is constant and a graph passes through the origin.

**Worked examples.** 36 bolts in 4 boxes gives 9 bolts/box, so 7 equal boxes hold 63. A pace of 3 meters/second for half a minute covers 3 × 30 = 90 meters, not 1.5.

**Watch for:** Inverting the ratio or mixing hours and minutes.

## Percentages

**Concept and strategy.** Identify the base before computing. Use 1 ± r for percent change and multiply successive changes.

**Worked examples.** A 30% reduction of $50 gives 0.70 × 50 = $35. A value of 96 after a 20% rise had original value 96/1.20 = 80; subtracting 20% of 96 uses the wrong base.

**Watch for:** Assuming a 20% decrease followed by a 20% increase returns to the start.

## Probability and conditional probability

**Concept and strategy.** Count favorable outcomes over equally likely possible outcomes. “Given” shrinks the sample space; without replacement, update both counts.

**Worked examples.** Of 15 volunteers, 6 can drive; of those 6, 4 work evenings. P(evening | can drive) = 4/6, not 4/15. In 3 red and 2 blue tokens, P(two reds without replacement) = (3/5)(2/4) = 3/10.

**Watch for:** Reversing the condition and treating two draws as independent after removal.

## One-variable data: Distributions and measures of center and spread

**Concept and strategy.** Sort for a median; divide the sum by count for a mean. Spread describes variation. A uniform shift changes center but not range or standard deviation.

**Worked examples.** 1, 3, 3, 13 has median 3 and mean 5. Adding 2 to every value gives 3, 5, 5, 15, with median 5 and the same range 12.

**Watch for:** Calling a value “the middle” without sorting and passing an extreme value’s entire increase through to the mean.

## Two-variable data: Models and scatterplots

**Concept and strategy.** Identify axes, units and what slope/intercept mean in context. Predicted values lie on the model, observed values on points. Residual = observed − predicted.

**Worked examples.** For model y = 2x + 5 and observed point (4, 11), the residual is 11 − 13 = −2. If x is years after 2020 and y is thousands of visits, the slope means 2,000 predicted additional visits per year, within the useful model range.

**Watch for:** Reversing residual sign or extrapolating far past observations without justification.

## Inference from sample statistics and margin of error

**Concept and strategy.** Identify the population actually sampled. For a reported estimate ± margin, compute endpoints in the same units; use random sampling to justify population estimates.

**Worked examples.** 36% ± 4 percentage points yields 32%–40%. In a representative random sample, 18% subscribe; for a population of 5,000, a point estimate is 900 subscribers, not a certainty.

**Watch for:** Mistaking a sample estimate for a guaranteed count or using a margin to fix a biased sample.

## Evaluating statistical claims: Observational studies and experiments

**Concept and strategy.** Random assignment supports causal comparisons among participants; random sampling supports generalization to the sampling population. Look for confounding and selection bias.

**Worked examples.** Randomly assigning volunteers to white or blue study lights can support a causal comparison within the trial, but volunteers may not represent all students. Observing that students who choose blue lights also study longer shows association, not proof of cause.

**Watch for:** Using “random” without saying whether it describes selection or assignment.

## Area and volume

**Concept and strategy.** Write a formula from the dimensions and track square or cubic units. For similar figures, area scales with length factor squared, volume with it cubed.

**Worked examples.** A cylinder with r = 3 and h = 7 has volume 63π. Scaling a 3-by-4 rectangle by a length factor of 2 gives a 6-by-8 rectangle with four times the area, 48 instead of 12.

**Watch for:** Using diameter as radius or subtracting an area from a volume.

## Lines, angles, and triangles

**Concept and strategy.** Use straight-line pairs (180°), vertical angles (equal), and triangle angle sums (180°). When using similarity, match corresponding vertices and sides.

**Worked examples.** Angles of 48° and 71° leave 61° for the third angle; its adjacent exterior angle is 119°, equal to 48° + 71°. If sides 5 and 8 correspond in similar triangles, a side of 10 in the first corresponds to 16 in the second.

**Watch for:** Inferring angle equality from a sketch when lines are not stated parallel or when side correspondence is unknown.

## Right triangles and trigonometry

**Concept and strategy.** Mark the hypotenuse first and choose the reference angle. Relative to that angle, sin = opposite/hypotenuse, cos = adjacent/hypotenuse, tan = opposite/adjacent.

**Worked examples.** Legs 7 and 24 give hypotenuse √(49 + 576) = 25. For the acute angle opposite 7, sine is 7/25 and tangent is 7/24. A 45°–45°–90° triangle with leg 6 has hypotenuse 6√2.

**Watch for:** Calling a side “opposite” without checking which acute angle is being used.

## Circles

**Concept and strategy.** Use (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r² for center (h, k). Use a central-angle fraction of the whole circle for sectors and arcs.

**Worked examples.** (x + 3)² + (y − 4)² = 25 has center (−3, 4) and radius 5. A 60° sector of radius 6 has area (1/6)(36π) = 6π and arc length (1/6)(12π) = 2π.

**Watch for:** Reading r² as r or using the arc-length formula when asked for sector area.
